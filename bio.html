RIZZIO BIOGRAPHY
Rizzio emerged from the early-90s UK hip-hop landscape as a constructed myth rather than a conventional artist. Across eight LPs and 125 tracks forming the Foundation Era, he built a closed-loop universe where persona, numerology, satire, geography, and sexual ritual converge into a deliberate architectural system. The work operates as structure, not accident. Each track functions as ritual. Each persona acts as mask and mirror. Each recurring motif reinforces coded design. What appears excessive is intentional; what appears chaotic is engineered repetition. At the center stands Rizzio — hero and antagonist, narrator and witness, seducer and strategist. From this core emerge Sir Rizz, The Rizla, Dirty Old Man, Game Show Host, Freak of the Week, and other alter-voices that fracture and recombine across the catalogue. The contradictions are not flaws; they are framework. The world is populated by archetypal women, desire, satire, vulnerability, dominance, emotional architecture and disappointment circulate through coded recurrence. Across Rizzio's landscape, the persona evolves from raw street charmer to executive authority to self-aware architect. By the tenth ritual invocation, the system seals. The persona no longer reacts to culture; it defines its own structure. Rizzio is not simply a rapper. He is an architect of persona and repetition. The Anthology stands as a coded, constructed body of work designed to outlive the performer and sustain the myth.
ASK YO' MOMMA (LP) (JUL 1993 – AUG 1993)

July 1993: Rizzio steps out onto the Tottenham High Road with the city blacked out, summer heat rising and blue lights cutting through the dark in “Close To The Borderline”. It is hardly a soft launch. “Amplifier Fire” then brings the domestic drama home: payday, the Sierra, and a “300 nicker” synthesizer that somehow becomes a household crisis. “Friends And Strangers” briefly puts tenderness back on the table, with a kiss on a sleeping partner’s cheek and a night that lasts until morning; then “California Flash” arrives in a purple sash, moustache apparently long enough to require its own postcode, and promptly steals the stage.

“Rizzio’s Rebirth” makes its position clear: no bearded baby is going to be his role model. “SABA And CKW” moves from football to 3-6-5 calls, bottles and a night of parties, while “Stiletto” turns attraction into a blood-price relationship: sliced, wounded, and still coming back for more. “Miss Understanding” drops the jokes long enough to ask what has happened to the family, children and community; after that comes the almost painfully simple question “Can I Be A Good Boy?” before “Down The Boulevard” introduces the crew—Ashley the ladies’ man, Steven with the plan, Kevin the brain, Bobby the wild card, Cornell looking sharp and Andrew reading the room.

The record collection then takes centre stage in “Legend Of My Record Collection (Wickedest Selection)”, tracing the sound back to the family vinyl, Aswad and “Concrete Slaveship”; “Tribute To Eddie Hazel” salutes the guitarist whose “Maggot Brain” and Funkadelic legacy made the six-string sound like a mind being let loose. “P Flex” arrives to spell out its own name and announce that the young rapper has started taking up space. Finally, “Here To A Special No Thanks Son Part One” plays its cards close to the chest: lessons have been learned, the light is still shining, and the rap is very definitely not done. A raw debut, then—but the blueprint is already peeking out from beneath the purple sash.

DON'T LOOK - BUY!! (LP) (SEP 1993 – JAN 1994)

Barely has the first suit been tailored before September brings “DON’T LOOK - BUY!!”. “Girl Called Sally” begins with a West End drive, a dinner invitation and a woman who wants a man who can meet her needs; the flirtation comes with a Thames Valley backstory and a refrain that leaves the narrator watching from a distance. “May-Kit-Fun-Key” pulls the curtain back on the studio: a couple of tunes, a backbeat, lyrics that might stick, and the grand ambition of turning the whole thing into a work of art. “Nasty Iz Nicety” wakes up beside a supposedly wonderful girl, then discovers that the difference between high and low can ring through your head like Big Ben. Romance, it seems, is not always sweet-smelling.

“Tottenham” plants the flag at N.15, where geography is easy to state but the maths of disadvantage is anything but simple. “What A Difference A Day Makes” turns a familiar phrase into a ridiculous chain of what-ifs—take, bake, eat the whole cake—and “Undercover Business” introduces a woman who can sell to the police and magistrate, extend credit until Friday and still keep the neighbourhood supplied. After the gleeful “Flyonchildren” interjection, “On The Beach Lies A Boy” relocates the action to a hot Los Angeles day, where a boy sends his romantic message by paper aeroplane and hopes it reaches its target. “OCU4A3T YI8R12B” then refuses to behave like a normal song at all: the phonetic puzzle gives way to a roll-call of P-Funk musicians and an instruction not to take it to the bridge—just fade.

“You Scared Me Baby” finds love frightened clean out of the room, with distrust lurking behind every step on the floor. In “In A Funny Way I Don’t Feel Sorry You’re Getting What You Served”, the narrator weighs the rules of proposition, discretion and consequences, then decides that some lessons are best learned the hard way. “Bbrand Nnew Pplayer” announces that the new player is here to stay, while “End Of The World (Not The)” turns a breakup into radio-listening, repeated thoughts and a ski-mask resolve. “Here To A Special No Thank You Part Two” closes the shop with a raised middle finger and a victory lap. September’s swagger has become a more complicated beast: romance, neighbourhood economics, studio self-mythology and revenge all share the same battered dancefloor.

LYRICAL CONTENT (LP) (FEB 1994 – SEP 1994)

By February 1994 the voice has grown louder, stranger and less interested in behaving. “Music Of The World” opens with a Kool & The Gang-flavoured approach to mackin’, before “A Mind Is A Terrible Thing To Waste” crashes in with its hijack wordplay and uncomfortable “Pork people” imagery. “69” returns to the backdoor-man narrative, with Gemini’s mask-and-gat imagery adding theatre to the sexual bravado; “Fear Of A Black Tenant” shifts the anger towards landlords and estate agents, making the housing market part of the confrontation. “Girl Won’t Even Notice (She Smells Bad)” then brings the argument down to the nose, the fish and chips and the everyday indignities of a woman who apparently will not notice the problem herself. After the brief “Zzzzzz (Interlude)”, “SuperRizzio (Save The Day!)” bursts in as a superhero with rippling biceps and a mission to rescue the UKK.

“Motorway Man” takes the pursuit onto the road, turning regional travel into a logistical catalogue of conquests; “End Of The World (U.S. Remix)” revisits the earlier heartbreak in a new outfit, still keeping the ski-mask mood. “Keep It Dark” calls for African names and navigation rather than simply accepting the labels handed down by others. “Placebo Pimp Hustlin’ Gangster” punctures the professional-prankster image, while “My Misses” files romantic mistakes into a year-by-year ledger of puns, misses and “Miss Take” disasters. “X-Rated” pushes the material into deliberately outrageous territory, and “Like Those Lovely Girls Say” lets the women’s own refrain become part of the performance. “Here To A Special No Thank Me Part Three” rounds everything up with the confidence of a man who thinks he can deliver the bone and the punchline in the same breath.

This is the album where the catalogue’s range becomes impossible to ignore: sexual comedy sits beside housing anger, identity and naming, superhero fantasy and the practical business of getting from one place to another. The shifts are abrupt by design. The same voice can be ridiculous in one bar and socially observant in the next, then return wearing a cape as though nothing unusual has happened.

I'LL GET MINE IN YO' HOME (LP) (OCT 1994 – APR 1995)

October 1994 finds Rizzio treating the home as a destination, a bargaining chip and sometimes an alternative to paying for a hotel. “Big Booty Bitches, Slutty Slags And Horny Hoes” opens with gold teeth and Lon-Town pride at full volume; the title track then explains the “con” with silky efficiency, while its narrator keeps his own digs safely off the map. “We’re Glad You’re Here” stages the adoring welcome of a groupie meeting an unorthodox star, before “Rappin’ Will Get You Nowhere On The Dancefloor” reminds would-be suitors that a two-step may do more than a speech. “Journey Through Da Lon-Town” makes the city itself the route—Tottenham, Brixton and the spaces in between—and, in the process, becomes an important early meeting point for Rizzio, Stone, Yinka and Gemini in the developing history that will later lead towards Project X.

“Phone Call Yall” brings the story back to the telephone, while “Queue Up All Y’all Pre-Funkadelics Peoples” turns the verse into a deep P-Funk roll-call: this is a fan who knows the family tree and intends to show his working. Then “Six O’Clock” darkens the mood with a set-up that is anything but a routine appointment. “Fiz Zong Haz Kno Tytal” deliberately makes nonsense part of the form; “Pointless Niggaz” dismisses those who bring no value, and “Ya Know Tha Drill” adds Mike Tyson-style force and an Irish-man gag to the pile-up. “V Riddla” introduces another mask, laser-focused on the puzzle of identity, before “Sir Rizz” gives the emerging title its direct, unmistakable name. The album signs off with “Here To A Special No Thank Your Part Four”, another end-of-chapter tally from a character who has no intention of handing over the keys.

The title may promise a home invasion, but the record’s real territory is broader: club etiquette, telephone connections, London geography, musical ancestry, danger and the construction of a public identity. The knight is not fully explained in one stroke; “Sir Rizz” is the named arrival, and later material will add the extra flourishes to the transformation.

COMIN' ROUNDA MOUNTAIN (LP) (MAY 1995 – FEB 1996)

“Can’t Stay Away” throws open the doors like a returning act stepping back under the lights. COMIN’ ROUNDA MOUNTAIN was conceived as a pseudo-live record, so the show-business swagger is part of the premise—but the crowd is not pasted over every track. “Wanton Streak” establishes the appetite, and “Cummin’ Round Her Mountain” takes the album title for a deliberately filthy ride. “Zany Maniac” turns eccentricity into a calling card; “Dirty Old Man In A Raincoat” introduces the character who will haunt later chapters, before “Favourite Word Is F (Part 1)” and “Vitamin F (Part 2)” turn one favourite expletive into a two-part nutritional supplement. “U No U Could B Good 2 U” offers a promise that may or may not survive the narrator’s own behaviour.

“Vinyl Vs C.D” stops the seduction to defend the twelve-inch record against the compact disc, insisting that culture should flip and spin rather than shrink. “Homiez All Knowz Me” brings the cabinet into view—Stone, Yinka, Gemini and DJ Def K—with Macbeth’s warnings hanging over the crew’s enemies. “Aroundaway” returns to the backdoor-man who can scout a line-up of women like a football manager but has no wish to hear tiny feet pattering around the house. “Death To The Stupid Salesman” takes a swing at the unwanted pitch; “Put On You Chocolate Stuff” turns vanilla and caramel into a grooming ritual, as if getting ready for a date requires the seriousness of a laboratory procedure. “Trajic Hour” and “Nothing Has Changed” then sober the room with their anger at systems described as “stink and unclean”, and the desire for a mini-revolution and a sanctuary for the Queen. The reprise of “Aroundaway”, now featuring P.H.2 aka Infra-Red, closes the loop with a different presence in the room.

At the end, “Here To A Special No Thank My Part Five” behaves like an encore and a round-up rather than just another song with a familiar name: the crowd gets its participation, the performer checks the audience is still there, and the catalogue gathers itself before the next climb. The record’s live-show illusion, funk inheritance and increasingly serious political voice coexist without pretending every track is a concert recording.

EQUALOPPORTUNITYNASTIES (LP) (MAR 1996 – NOV 1996)

By March 1996, the opening “Here To A Special No Thank His Part Six” has Rizzio checking the Cap’m’s Log while injustice blooms in the higher towers. The jokes are still loaded, but the pressure outside the window is becoming harder to laugh away. “Renegades”, featuring Cynthia and Debbie with Project X, brings the blue sirens and institutional racism into focus; “This Goes Out To The Lon-Town” then becomes a guided tour from the dark scene around Finsbury Park to Brixton’s honeys, stairwells and subway grit. “Ashley’s Goin’ Church!” marks a change in Ashley’s path, leaving the ski-mask image behind for salvation, while “Cap’m’s Log” turns back to the notebook to take aim at “Sterile Bulls”, “London Potty” rappers and all-mouth-no-trousers pretenders.

The personal pursuits resume with “Nikki’s Nikkers” and its “sexy wonder bras”, followed by “Comin’ 4 U”, where warp factor two and Teletext headlines turn the chase into science-fiction tabloid news. “Wanted” makes the narrator a villain wanted by women across the country; “PX Reps” lays out the crew’s territory and separates the real from the fake. “Cautious My Precious” throws metric hash into the Tekken universe, with Yoshimitsu’s sword providing the sharp end of the joke. “Fisherman’s Friend” reels in its catch with fishing-magazine language, and “Necessary Necessities” adds Josie D’Arby and ice-cold milk to the list of essential supplies. Then the levity cracks: “Injustice” points to pills, jabs and the treatment of people in mental-health systems, while “Odds Are Stacked Against Me” sees three-piece suits rigging the deck against Black youth.

“Man Of The Match” responds with the “law of sod” and an improbable ghostly haberdashery of style; “Untitled” sends its message to intelligent minds from a loft out of sight, with Yinka keeping the girlies organised and the queue moving. Finally, “Here To A Special Thank Heavens Part Seven” gives the recurring end-piece a spiritual turn. The album moves between political indictment and outrageous mack talk, but the sequence matters: the party is still going, even as the logbook records exactly what is wrong with the world outside.

DREAD GROOVES HORIZONTAL (LP) (DEC 1996 – APR 1997)

If EQUALOPPORTUNITYNASTIES kept one foot in the street and one in the political argument, DREAD GROOVES HORIZONTAL arrives in December 1996 with a late-night executive swagger. “Here To A Special No Thanks Mate Part Eight” rejects fake smiles and sets the tone: this is a man who claims to have built his own from his ends. “So You Wanna Be A Boxer?” takes a swing at Frank Bruno’s defeat by Mike Tyson, reducing the golden-boy image to ice water in the ring. “Night-Mare Animal Instincts” turns the lights down for a horror-core parade of Funkadelic, the Dirty Old Man and Macbeth, before a grizzly-bear encounter with Beth. “House Of Lordz” drags city commotion into the highest court, name-checks George Clinton and Geraldo, and has Sir Rizz claiming he has been hired to put the record straight.

“Metaphors 4 Betta Scores” converts cricket and football into bedroom commentary—Keith Piper, maidens, Cantona and John Barnes all get drafted into the same match. “Defy The Laws Of Physics” pushes the sporting boast further with Andy Cole and a scoring record that apparently ignores gravity; “I Will Never Never Surrender” turns persistence into a vow, while “Red Blue (Colour Me Funky)” makes colour and groove share the same palette. Track 100, “Dicey”, brings the gambling and card imagery into sharp focus. “Projex In FX Iz PX” then lays out the crew as a secret-agent cartoon, with Yinka, Stone, Infra-Red, General Flex, DJ Def and Sir Rizz fighting the evils of Baron Silas Greenback from a Baker Street base. “Pack Neva Stacks” takes the gang poolside in August, with the crew and a school of honeys turning the day into a collective operation.

“Sho’ ’N’ ’Tel (Shona & Tallulah)” advises keeping the cards close and the plans quieter than the rumours. “Confucius Says” twists the old sayings until Rizzio can declare that when he is not himself, he is himself; “I Don’t Make No Erm-Ology” parades archaeology, mythology, biology and psychology alongside the NVQs and college knowledge. “Bring Your Dinner Too” swaps bedroom management for a furious football-club dressing-room tirade about discipline and who runs the show. “The Executive Seducer” moves the same authority into boardrooms, where a glance signs deals and a handshake hides the pressure. “Here To A Special No Thank Mine Part Nine” closes with the familiar roll-call of women, lap-sitting and whispered requests. The curb has become a penthouse—but the old streetwise instincts have come upstairs too.

THE PROJECT-X ERA — THE AMERICANS SET THE FOUNDATION (LP) (JUL 1995 – OCT 1996)

Project X is a collective, and this album’s dates overlap the solo Rizzio chronology because the group was developing alongside it—not because every earlier collaborator should be retrospectively labelled a member. The narrated introduction to “Bad Lyrics” presents the world-famous Project X, then Stone asks for a second or two to get through while the crew rounds up the masses and whips lyrics, not lashes. “Tings And Stuff” turns the question “who got the tings, who got the stuff?” into a call-and-response, with Kandace and General Flex helping the identity stick. “Ghetto Street Stuff” gives Yinka the Thinker and Juliet space to salute the crew and give women their due; “Desperate Times” shifts the focus to brothers and sisters weighing poverty against mortality, with crime, pollution and policing making the situation feel lived rather than merely performed.

“Know Tha Game” establishes the card-table language—hedged bets, a frantic city, straight flushes and the Ace of Spades—before “C Da Real” challenges the card-sharp and the rude boy to face reality, rather than hiding behind drink or a hard-man pose. “Playtime” returns to money, Mercedes, weed, wine, pearls and black diamonds, making the hedonism collective; “U Get Me” pushes the PX/XP identity into fresh-cut gear, cash and getting paid by the verse. “Bangin’” opens with the big five African carnivores and the struggle between predator and prey, then lets the group’s voices inhabit that survival frame. “You’re Goin’ Down You Slagg” brings Yinka and Gemini into the rough, rugged crew attack, while “Who Da Baddest” asks who is maddest and baddest before warning about the tragedies born from thuggery and trickery. The “Sir Rizz Mixx” of “Know Tha Game” returns to the lucky card and the same rough streets, now with Juliet’s voice threaded through the wager.

This is not a solo Rizzio LP with a few guests added around the edges. Its voices, exchanges and shared refrains are the point: the American blueprint is taken up by a Lon-Town group, and the question of who holds the winning card becomes a question of how the crew functions together.

TOTTENHAM IN DA LON-TOWN 2097 (EP) (MAY 1997 – JUN 1997)

The final double-sided EP looks back over the road just travelled, but it does not simply park the van and admire the view. “C Da Real Pt 2” re-enters the game with Tekken and Street Fighter kicking off in the same breath, with your girlfriend's sister's favourite cousin then reasserts the number-one spot with the ladies apparently ready to make Rizzio their spouse. “You’re In The Army Now” turns the boast into military strategy: formations, ambushes, a score of 83 to 3 and a narrator who insists he prefers Marilyn McCoo over Marilyn Monroe whilst hustling through the night. The campaign gives way to “In The Dead Of Night” a literal dark blue blanket that covers Rizzio like a satin sheet that peels away from the sky and cloaks him as he glides like a Mercedes in the Tottenham streets as the Dirty Old Man in his Raincoat slips out on his nocturnal mission.

Then comes “You Don’t Know Me”, and the swagger finally meets a consequence it cannot rhyme away. The 'Wannabe' bad-boy gangster wants to see himself in the Haringey Advertiser, talks big on the block but the courtroom then strips away the swagger: the judge says “LIFE! The newspaper did not report that the next track would unknowingly be his last....

“Here To A Special No Thanks Then Part Ten” closes the Rizzio Era by refusing applause as the final measure. Rizzio has “seen every trick in the spectrum”, corrects rather than depends, and declares himself the rule rather than the question; The “No Thanks” ending is therefore more than a repeated title: it gathers the catalogue, looks at the myths it has built, and leaves the last word with the architect, "Amen!". Tottenham and da Lon-Town have supplied the streets, the cast, the jokes and the warnings. The story ends in 1997, but the title points the imagination towards 2097 — and leaves the door open just long enough for the echo to get through.
